The problem: alarms say something is wrong, not which repair to book

A drifting pump produces thousands of readings. Thresholds fire, people decide, and what they learned stays in their heads. An AI model on every reading costs money per reading and turns every flicker into a decision someone has to trust. Hand-written rules never keep up. Tamoz is the middle path: watch with fixed rules, think only when it is worth it, act only inside fixed limits, and learn from repairs that were checked.

How it works: the AI proposes, fixed policy decides

  1. Watch. Readings arrive with their own timestamps and a quality flag. Tamoz tracks each machine against its own normal.
  2. Think only when worth it. A fixed scheduler, not an AI model, starts one bounded diagnosis when something has materially changed.
  3. Propose and check. The model returns what it saw, what it infers, how sure it is and what it suggests. Fixed policy checks risk, hourly caps, approvals and software safety checks. The model never sets power or timing.
  4. Act and verify. The command is sent once and cannot repeat by accident. The outcome comes back as independent evidence.
  5. Learn. If the same repair holds three times, the model drafts a rule. It is replayed over history, and a person approves it.
The AI proposes what should happen. Fixed policy decides how much, and whether. The device's own built-in limit decides when to stop.

A learned rule stays on a short leash: it acts only inside the range of readings it learned from, only on low-risk actions, and only after a watch-only trial in which it shows what it would do while the AI still decides. It switches itself off if the device model changes, an audit disagrees or its action stops working.

What it did in the test

A simulator ran eight pumps and injected faults on a schedule only it knew. Every fault had a deadline: book the right maintenance before it and the pump recovers, miss it and the pump is damaged. The faults were bearing wear, misalignment, sensor drift (a healthy pump whose probe reads high) and a cavitation look-alike that settles by itself and needs no action. Once the system had three checked misalignment repairs, it asked the model to write the pattern down as a rule:

If the pump's axial vibration over the last 15 minutes is at least 1.3 times its own 24-hour average, radial vibration and current have not risen, and the sensor heartbeat is present: book alignment maintenance.

Replayed over history, this rule would have fired 3 times and agreed 3 times. A person approved it, it ran in the watch-only trial until it had agreed three times, and it became active during the second learning day. Then the same three test days were run twice from the same starting database:

Boundary: what this does not show

Read before you rely on it

One simulated station, one test scenario, three test days, one AI model, pre-release software. It shows the learning loop works end to end. It does not show what it would save on your equipment.

  • Only one of three fault types earned a rule. The approver rejected all three bearing-wear drafts because each replay disagreed once. Sensor drift never became a rule because the model always called it bearing wear.
  • The rule answers in about a millisecond, but from reading to recommendation a rule case took 147 seconds at the 95th percentile: the scheduler waits a minute to confirm a change is real, then queues behind AI diagnoses.
  • The approver was a scripted, careful approver, not a person with a stake. The physical side is a small fan bench, not a plant: a governed command made its motor spin and I watched it, but did not measure it.
  • No field deployment and no safety certification. Certified safety stays in external systems by design. Other settings are labelled one by one on the use-cases page.

Fixed since the trial. A repair now counts as verified only if the reading returns close to its pre-repair baseline (checked on the trial's own records: it rejects all 9 sensor-drift cases that had been counted as repairs and keeps all 16 genuine ones). A lost acknowledgement no longer blocks a pump. The numbers above do not include either fix.

What a pilot would look like

This is a proposal. It has not been done with anyone yet, and terms are to be discussed. A first pilot is advisory first, measured against criteria we agree up front.

  1. Rehearse. You share past sensor data from a few machines. I replay it and show what Tamoz would have flagged and recommended. Nothing touches your equipment.
  2. Advise. Tamoz reads a live, read-only copy of the stream and recommends repairs beside your team. Nobody has to act on them. Every recommendation is logged with its evidence.
  3. Act, only if you choose. Low-risk, reversible actions with a named approver. Learned rules start only after you approve them, in a watch-only trial first.

What I would ask of you: a read-only copy of the sensor stream for a few machines of one type; your known fault types, with the usual repair and deadline for each; and a named person who approves rules.

What you get: the measured results for each use case, and every decision with its evidence. Duration and cost are part of the terms to be discussed. Which model runs, and where your data goes, is decided with you before anything starts. See the use cases for what a pilot would measure.

Where it came from

Most agent demos look great until they meet the edges: the same side effect fires twice, a crash leaves things half-done, a model acts on a sensor that died an hour ago. In Tamoz that is the starting requirement. The code is in three MIT-licensed public repositories: Tamoz, Agentic Stream and Streams Simulator. The trial work is on their rule-promotion branches, not yet merged to main.
