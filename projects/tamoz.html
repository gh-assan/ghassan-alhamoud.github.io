The problem: alarms say something is wrong, not which repair to book

The problem I keep seeing is this. Alarms tell you something is wrong. Nobody tells you which repair to book, or whether last time's repair worked.

  • A drifting pump produces thousands of readings. Thresholds fire, people decide, and what they learned stays in their heads.
  • Putting an AI model on every reading costs money per reading and makes every flicker a decision someone has to trust.
  • Hand-written rules are exact but never keep up, and an AI that acts on equipment needs limits it cannot talk its way past.

Tamoz is an attempt at the middle path: watch deterministically, think only when it is worth it, act only inside fixed limits, and learn from repairs that were checked. Cost follows change, not traffic.

Design: the model proposes, fixed policy disposes

On this page the whole system is called Tamoz. It has three parts: a streaming runtime that watches and governs (Agentic Stream), an agent framework that does the reasoning (Tamoz), and a simulated world to test against (Streams Simulator). The path of one decision:

  1. Read. Sensor readings arrive with their own timestamps and a quality flag.
  2. Summarise. The stream layer folds them into one sealed snapshot of what the sensors say right now (a “Situation”).
  3. Decide whether thinking is worth it. A scheduler, not a model, starts one bounded AI diagnosis only when something has materially changed.
  4. Propose and check. The model returns a typed proposal: what it saw, what it infers, how sure it is, what action it suggests. Policy checks risk class, rate limits, approvals, device authority and interlocks. The model never sets power or timing.
  5. Act and verify. The command goes out once, with an idempotency key. The outcome comes back as independent evidence.
The model decides what should happen. Policy decides how much. Firmware decides when to stop.

That split is why I am comfortable pointing an agent at equipment at all. The first time I ran a real model on a cold-box snapshot it reasoned correctly and then tried to write a duty cycle of 50% on its own. The whole decision was rejected. That rejection is the design working, not failing. The system is not tied to one model: it works with any model, and the trial below used one.

Evidence: a pump station that learned its repairs

Evidence, in a simulated pump station. The simulator runs eight pumps. Every few seconds each pump reports radial and axial vibration, temperature, motor current and speed. The simulator injects faults on a schedule only it knows, and every fault has a deadline: book the right maintenance before it and the pump recovers, miss it and the pump is damaged. A test run is 30 simulated hours with up to six faults plus one look-alike.

Bearing wear

Radial vibration creeps up over hours. Needs bearing maintenance within about 13 hours.

Misalignment

Axial vibration rises while the radial probe stays quiet. Needs alignment work within about 12 hours.

Sensor drift

The pump is healthy but its probe reads high. Needs recalibration within 2 hours. A threshold cannot tell it from wear.

Cavitation look-alike

A short rise that settles on its own. The right answer is to do nothing.

The rule it learned

If the pump's axial vibration over the last 15 minutes is at least 1.25 times its own 24-hour average, radial vibration and current have not risen, and the sensor heartbeat is present: book alignment maintenance.

Replayed over history, this rule would have fired 3 times, agreed 3 times and disagreed 0 times. It was approved, ran in shadow beside the model until it had agreed three times, and became active during the second learning day.

Its safety leash

  • It acts only inside the range of readings it learned from, widened by 10% of the values. Outside that range the case goes to the model.
  • It may recommend low-risk actions only. Stopping a pump stays with people.
  • A disagreement keeps it in shadow.
  • An automatic switch-off turns it off if the device model changes, an audit disagrees, or its action stops working.

Read this carefully. Two fewer model calls and one more repair on time is small, and the bearing-wear difference comes from the model's own variation, because no bearing rule existed. What the rule changed is who decided the misalignment cases: a rule, in about a millisecond, with nothing to pay per decision. The sixth misalignment went unrepaired in both runs because a simulated lost acknowledgement left that pump waiting for an operator. That has been fixed since.

The first time, the rule never acted

The first full run learned the same rule and it never fired. Its safe range was 0.016 wide (2.747 to 2.763), so every new pump fell just outside it, and an hourly booking limit read from the wall clock refused bookings when 30 simulated hours ran in about eight minutes. After two design changes the range became 2.47 to 3.04 and the limit follows event time. Rule decisions went from 0 to 5. I include this because it shows how the system fails and what that cost to find.

Scorecard in one line: five of the planned pass conditions passed, two failed (model calls per handled fault: 76% against a target of 50% or less; rule decision latency: 147 s at the 95th percentile), and two were not exercised. The table is in the technical deep dive, which also has a walkthrough video (watch it there). The full result is in RESULT.md on the rule-promotion branch of the Streams Simulator repository; that work is not yet merged to main.

A rehearsal room for plants

The simulator began as a scored test world with a sealed answer key. It now also speaks the device link, so it can stand in for a device. That makes it a place to rehearse before touching equipment:

  • run a simulated copy of a device before the hardware exists;
  • put a real board beside simulated ones in front of the system as one plant;
  • let an operator hold, approve or stop commands at that point;
  • record a session and replay it byte for byte;
  • compare the simulated copy with what the real board reported.

A visual workspace, the canvas, lets you see the plant, build one from a component library, run it, disturb it by injecting faults, and review what happened: readings, command stages, connection health. It needs a desktop browser at least 1280 px wide.

Honest limit. The real-board path is built and tested against a stand-in gateway; I have not yet run it with a real board in this setup. The real hardware I have is the small fan bench described in the technical deep dive.

Boundary: what this does not show

Read before you rely on it

One simulated station, one seed, three test days. It shows the learning loop works end to end. It does not show what it would save on your equipment.

  • Only one of three fault families earned a rule. Bearing wear got three draft rules and the approver rejected all three because each replay disagreed once. Sensor drift never became a rule because the model always called it bearing wear.
  • The rule itself takes about a millisecond, but from situation to recommendation a rule case took 147 s at the 95th percentile, because it waits out a one-minute debounce and queues behind model calls.
  • The model-call saving is small (13 against 15) because only one family has a rule.
  • The approver in the trial was a scripted, careful approver, not a person with a stake. There has been no run with a human approver yet.
  • Pre-release software; no field deployment; no safety certification. The physical side is a fan bench, not a plant. Other domains (aquaculture, cold chain) were run only in the earlier simulator rounds with a scripted reasoner, and chilled-water plants have not been run at all.

Fixed since the trial. A repair now counts as verified only if the reading returns close to its pre-action baseline (checked against the trial data: it rejects all 9 sensor-drift cases and keeps all 16 genuine repairs). And a lost acknowledgement no longer blocks a pump. These two landed after the final run, so the numbers above do not include them.

One more correction, from the first five simulator rounds: the reasoning step was scripted. I rebuilt the evidence path so model calls go through a witness gateway that records the exact bytes. The full account is in the technical deep dive.

What a pilot would look like

This is a proposal. It has not been done with anyone yet. A first pilot is advisory first, measured against criteria we agree up front.

  1. Rehearse. Replay a sample of your sensor history through the system and, where useful, build a simulated copy of the device. Nothing touches equipment.
  2. Advise. It runs beside your people on a live, read-only feed and proposes repairs. Nobody acts on them unless they choose to. Every proposal and its evidence is logged.
  3. Act within bounds. Low-risk, reversible actions with a named approver, and learned rules only after your approval, in shadow first.

What I would ask of you: a read-only copy of the sensor stream for a few machines of one type; your list of known fault types, the repair for each and its deadline; and a named person who approves rules. What you get: the measured results per use case, and every decision with its evidence. Terms are to be discussed. See the use cases for what a pilot would measure.

Where it came from

Most agent demos look great right up until they meet the edges. The same side effect fires twice, a crash leaves things half-done, a model acts on a sensor that died an hour ago, and afterwards nobody can say what happened or why. I got tired of treating that as cleanup for later. In Tamoz it is the starting requirement.

The code lives in three MIT-licensed, public repositories. The trial work is on their rule-promotion branches and is not yet merged to main.

  • Tamoz (Ruby): the agent framework: reasoning, memory, durable execution, evaluation.
  • Agentic Stream (Go): the always-on side: event time, Situations, the scheduler, policy, actions and the learned-rule playbook.
  • Streams Simulator (Go and a React canvas): the test world and rehearsal room.
