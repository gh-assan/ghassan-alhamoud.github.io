An agent for the physical world

Most AI agents live in a chat window. They know what you tell them, when you tell them. The world I care about doesn't wait to be asked. A cold room drifts warm at three in the morning. A pump bearing starts to wear. A sensor quietly stops reporting. Tamoz is built for that world. It reads what the sensors are saying, works out what is actually going on, and decides what should happen next.

Here's a concrete case. A cold box should hold 4 °C. It reads 8 °C and is still rising. The door hasn't opened, the room around it is a normal 20 °C, and the sensors report healthy. Tamoz gets that picture as one sealed, versioned snapshot called a Situation, and it answers the questions a good engineer on call would ask. Is this real or a blip? Does something else explain it? Is it worth acting on, and how sure am I? Its answer is a typed Decision: what it observed, what it infers, how confident it is, which evidence it used, and what it proposes. In this case, the answer is a genuine, sustained excursion, so it recommends bounded cooling.

Then comes the part I care about most. Tamoz decides what should happen. It never gets to decide how much. The cooling proposal has exactly one field the model can write, a mode of either hold or bounded_cooling. The duty cycle, the run time, the lease, and the safe stop belong to deterministic policy and to the device firmware, every time. The first time I ran this against a real model, it reasoned correctly and then tried to write duty_pct: 50 on its own. The whole decision was rejected. That rejection is the design working, not failing.

The model decides what should happen. Policy decides how much. Firmware decides when to stop.

That split is why I'm comfortable pointing an agent at equipment at all. The model's judgment is useful and fallible. So the things that can hurt someone (power, timing, interlocks, the emergency stop) sit in layers the model can't reach. Tamoz doesn't even know what hardware exists: a test in the repo fails the build if a serial port, GPIO pin, or actuator name ever appears in its production code.

Why stream-native matters

There are two obvious ways to connect an AI agent to live data, and both break down.

A chat agent you poll only sees the world when someone thinks to ask. A model on every event sees everything, but its cost grows with traffic, and it turns every flicker in the data into a decision somebody has to trust. Tamoz takes a third route together with its sibling project, Agentic Stream. A deterministic stream does the watching. The model does the thinking, and only when the situation has materially changed. Here's what that buys, with numbers from the simulator runs:

  • Cost follows change, not traffic. A deterministic scheduler decides when reasoning is worth paying for, using a trigger, a score threshold, debounce, cooldown, and coalescing of duplicate work. A quiet stretch costs nothing.
  • Time is handled properly. Readings carry the time they happened, not the time they arrived. Watermarks, windows, and lateness are explicit, so a reading that shows up an hour late updates the right moment instead of the wrong one.
  • Silence counts as a signal. When a sensor stops talking, the situation becomes uncertain, and risky actions fail closed. In one run, an oxygen probe in a fish pond went quiet, and the emergency water exchange was denied rather than guessed at.
  • The agent reasons over a frozen snapshot. Each episode is bound to one immutable, digest-checked Situation version. If the world moves on mid-thought, stale work is cancelled. And if a late correction shows an earlier action was wrong, the system reconsiders and can walk it back with a compensating action.
  • Anything can be replayed without touching anything. The same trace reproduces the same Situation history. Replay is built without any effectors, so I can re-run a bad night or shadow-test a new prompt without moving a single motor.
  • Storms stay contained. A cost ceiling and a kill switch stop new reasoning while ingestion keeps running, so a flood of alarms can't become a flood of model calls.

One decision, end to end

Here's the path a single reading takes, from a sensor to a moving part and back into memory. The technical deep dive draws each step in detail.

  1. Sense. A reading arrives with its device time and a quality flag. Malformed frames are quarantined, not forwarded.
  2. Situate. Agentic Stream folds the reading into the current Situation and publishes a new immutable version with a canonical digest.
  3. Admit. The cognitive scheduler decides whether this version deserves reasoning at all. Most don't.
  4. Understand. Tamoz receives the sealed snapshot over gRPC and re-checks its digest before any model call. It can pull more evidence only through a fixed list of read-only tools, inside a budget of time, calls, tokens, and cost.
  5. Decide. It returns a typed Decision that keeps facts apart from inferences, cites its evidence, states its uncertainty, and proposes zero or more intents from a catalog it can't extend.
  6. Govern. The stream checks the Decision belongs to the exact episode, attempt, and snapshot. Then policy re-checks every intent against the current state: freshness, risk class, approvals, rate limits, interlocks. Risky intents wait for a signed human approval.
  7. Act. The command goes into a durable outbox with an idempotency key. The dispatcher re-validates it right before the effect, and the firmware enforces the lease and the ceiling. If the result can't be proven, it's recorded as unknown and never blindly retried.
  8. Learn. The reconciled outcome comes back as independent evidence. Tamoz stores it as an Experience marked observed, and the next time a similar situation shows up, it recalls what happened.

Here's what's there today. Each status says whether you can run it, whether it's wired but deliberately held back, or whether it's still being built. A design doc isn't a feature.

One sealed Situation per episode, a digest checked before any model call, read-only evidence tools, and hard budgets on time, calls, tokens, and cost.

Typed intents from a catalog bound to the spec, risk classes R0 to R4, and a short list of model-writable fields. Agentic Stream re-checks all of it in Go.

Risky intents wait for a person. In the simulator rounds, approvals were ed25519-signed, single-use, and bound to the exact situation version. Telegram works as an operator channel, and approving over it is deny-only for now.

Shipped

Crash-safe execution

Every turn is a graph over SQLite checkpoints. It survives kill -9, resumes from the last committed step, and stops an ambiguous side effect as unknown for a human to settle.

Shipped

Memory that learns from outcomes

Experience, Knowledge, and Wisdom are kept as separate layers. An Experience is written only from an independently observed outcome, and a second occurrence of a fault recalled the first in four simulated domains.

Shipped

Evaluation framework

The release audit is regenerated by running each named test. Scorecards have hard-zero safety gates. A capability eval proves its own graders can tell a good agent from a bad one before it reports a score.

Shipped

Replay and shadow modes

Deterministic replay of the stream, recorded and shadow runs against frozen snapshots, and counterfactual runs that can reach only a simulator. None of them can touch a production effector.

Shipped

Governed tools

Local tools, skills, MCP servers, and web search are registered once and sealed. A skill or tool description can ask for more power, but it can never grant itself any.

Wired, gated

Self-healing

Every failed turn is classified against a typed failure model on every live path. The remediation engine is built and tested, but no repair rule has climbed the ladder from replay to active yet, so for now it observes and doesn't repair.

Wired, gated

Self-improvement

It generates candidate heuristics, scores them on a holdout the candidate never sees, and promotes one only with a human's approval, through tamoz improve promote. It never rewrites itself live.

In progress

Active investigation

When data is missing, Tamoz abstains today. The next step is a bounded loop that notices the gap, asks for the specific reading, and then decides.

In progress

Evidence-fitness gate in the agent

Refusing to recommend an action when the evidence is stale or out of range. Today only the downstream dispatcher enforces this. My own eval shows Tamoz's layer catching 0 of 8 unfit cases, and I've left that result public on purpose.

Five rounds in a simulated world

Before touching hardware, I tested the whole system against Streams Simulator, a deterministic world I wrote for this job. It generates realistic sensor streams from domains described in plain JSON: rotating machinery, fish ponds, water networks, cold-chain containers, shipments, and more. It breaks delivery on purpose (late, duplicated, missing), keeps a sealed record of what really happened, and scores the system's verdict against that truth afterwards. The system under test never sees the answer key.

I ran it in rounds, each with a written bar that had to be met with live evidence. Whatever broke got logged as an issue with a test before it was fixed. One thing up front, explained properly below: in these rounds the reasoning step was scripted. What they test is everything around the reasoning.

  1. Round 113 Aug 2026Pass
    Connect three systems that had never met

    A simulated pump with a wearing bearing streamed 31,683 events into Agentic Stream. What came out the other end was a real episode, an accepted Decision, a succeeded command, a reconciled outcome, and the first Experience Tamoz ever admitted as observed. Sixteen integration gaps turned up and got fixed on the way, including digest formats that disagreed between Ruby and Go.

  2. Round 214 Aug 2026Pass
    Make it supervised, and make it learn

    Budgets killed runaway episodes mid-flight. Superseded work was cancelled. A late reading triggered a reconsideration that downgraded an earlier action with a compensating one. A high-risk intent waited for a human, and a single trace ID ran from sensor event to outcome. The milestone: a second motor with the same fault recalled what happened to the first.

  3. Round 314 Aug 20262 pass1 partial
    Three industries: aquaculture, water, greenhouse

    The full arc ran live twice. A quiet baseline admitted nothing. A silent probe failed closed. An ambiguous signal got a watch instead of a guess. A post-feeding oxygen dip was correctly explained away. An emergency water exchange waited for a signed approval. A late correction walked back an intervention. And a second pond recalled the first. The greenhouse hit a real wall: at 8 bays by 9 channels, intents went stale before a human could approve them.

  4. Round 4preparedNot run yet
    Find the single-node ceiling

    The greenhouse wall raised the question of how many entities one node can reason about before timing breaks. Round 4 is a 256-to-1,024 battery-rack scenario built to find that ceiling. It's scaffolded and waiting its turn.

  5. Round 517 Aug 2026Pass1 engine bug
    Audit the assumptions: cold chain and logistics

    A reefer container went dark for 14 hours at sea, and 840 readings arrived late in one burst. The engine admitted them and reconsidered, but it found a real bug: a running total can't be revised by late corrections yet. In logistics, the decision named the true cause of a delay (a customs hold, at 0.9 confidence), refused to trust an optimistic carrier ETA, and recalled the previous shipment. A hub-outage storm of 1,105 events started zero episodes.

The correction I had to make

Reading the reports back, I found the flaw in my own proof. In all five rounds, the reasoning step was a scripted brain: a deterministic graph that produced the right diagnosis and even emitted "model started" events without calling a model. So the rounds prove the machinery (timing, governance, approvals, memory, recovery). They don't prove intelligence.

So I rebuilt the evidence path. Model calls now go through a witness gateway that signs a record of the exact bytes sent and received. The worker refuses to serve a scripted answer on any production route. The action catalog is bound to the spec and verified independently in Go. And a frozen benchmark protocol with 14 control checks has to pass before any intelligence number counts.

Then a real motor

Simulation tells you whether the logic holds. Hardware tells you whether you were honest about it. I wired the system to an Arduino-compatible board with a temperature sensor and a small fan motor. One program, a gateway, is the only thing allowed to open the USB port. The firmware exposes exactly one bounded action, set_pwm_lease on fan-01, capped at 60% duty with a 10-second lease and a local watchdog that cuts power when the lease runs out.

The first result was a perfect trace and a dead motor. The board accepted the command, reported it executed, and answered a state query with energized: true. The fan didn't move. A throwaway sketch driving the driver at full power in both directions: still nothing. Touching the motor straight to the supply: it spun at once. The fault was two jumpers. The driver's direction inputs sat on pins D4 and D3, while the firmware drove D6 and D7, so the H-bridge sat in its brake state however hard the enable line was pushed. I moved two wires, changed no code, and the next governed command spun the motor.

Separately, I gave a real model the cold-box snapshot. It reasoned its way to cooling, reached for the PWM value, and got rejected, as described above. With the catalog narrowed to a single mode field, the same model on the same snapshot returned mode: bounded_cooling and not one PWM digit.

The lesson I took away is the one the whole project is built on: energized: true is a statement about a pin, not a shaft. A receipt isn't an effect, and a green trace isn't a turning motor. I wrote up the full debugging story in When an AI Agent Met a Real Motor.

Why I built it, and what's next

Most agent demos look great right up until they meet the edges. The same side effect fires twice, a crash leaves things half-done, a model acts on a sensor that died an hour ago, and afterwards nobody can say what happened or why. I got tired of treating all that as cleanup for later. In Tamoz it's the starting requirement. Effects are assumed to run at least once unless I can prove otherwise, and anything genuinely ambiguous stops and asks a person rather than quietly retrying.

I built it in the least exciting order on purpose: evaluation before behavior, deterministic execution before persistence, persistence before anything model-driven. That makes it slower to demo than most frameworks. The payoff is that you can look inside and see where the edges are, including the ones I haven't fixed.

Next, in order: join the reviewed seam between Tamoz's intent and the stream's device route, and put a real instrument on the motor. Then run the Round 4 scale test to find the single-node ceiling. And close my own eval findings: an evidence-fitness gate inside the agent, and a bounded loop for going to find missing data instead of just abstaining.

The code lives in three MIT-licensed repositories:

  • Tamoz (Ruby): the agent. It handles understanding, decisions, memory, healing, improvement, and evaluation, as 27 independently installable gems plus the Tamoz Agent reference app.
  • Agentic Stream (Go): the always-on side. It covers event time, Situations, the cognitive scheduler, policy, and the action plane.
  • Streams Simulator (Go): the test world, with data-defined domains, delivery faults, sealed truth, and independent scoring.
