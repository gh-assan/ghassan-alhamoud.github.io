An AI agent that follows what is happening in the physical world.

Tamoz works with sensor streams from equipment and processes. It reasons about changes, proposes a response, and stores evidence about the outcome. A separate stream layer handles monitoring, timing, and the rules that govern actions.

What Tamoz is built for

A cold room warms up overnight. Equipment starts drawing more power. A sensor stops reporting. Each situation needs context before someone can decide whether to investigate, wait, or act.

Tamoz is designed to reason when the available data shows a meaningful change. The stream layer keeps watching between those changes. This avoids asking a model to interpret every reading and lets the agent work from a consistent view of the situation.

The intended applications include monitoring, alerts, investigation, and recommendations across equipment and processes. Benefits such as lower energy use or less material waste would need to be measured in an operational pilot.

Three systems working together

The system is three programs with separate jobs: one watches and governs, one reasons, and one tests the other two.

  • Agentic Stream runs continuously. It records sensor events with their timing and quality, publishes each situation as a fixed version, and calls the agent only when reasoning is useful. It also owns every action: policy checks a proposal again just before dispatch, commands and their outcomes are recorded durably, and approved rules learned from earlier repairs run here without a model call.
  • Tamoz does the reasoning, one bounded episode at a time. It works with any model, receives one fixed situation and a budget, can read further evidence through read-only tools an operator has granted, and returns a structured proposal with its uncertainty. It can also draft a rule from verified repairs for review. Its work is checkpointed so it recovers after a crash, and it has no direct path to equipment.
  • Streams Simulator is the test instrument. It generates sensor streams, can delay or corrupt their delivery, responds to commands, and keeps a sealed record of what really happened, so results are scored against that record. Runs can be replayed from a seed. Its visual canvas builds plants from a component library, runs them, and injects faults mid-run. A real board can also join simulated devices in one recorded session. It is not a physics engine or an industrial controller.

Together they form one loop, from readings to outcomes.

How a decision works

Consider a cold box that should hold 4 °C but reads 8 °C and is still warming. A reading alone does not explain whether the door was opened, the room became warmer, or a probe is unreliable.

  1. Build the situation. Combine the readings, their timing, and quality into a fixed snapshot of the available evidence.
  2. Investigate. Give the agent that snapshot and a budget for reasoning and permitted evidence tools.
  3. Propose. Record what was observed, what the agent infers, its uncertainty, and the response it suggests.
  4. Check. Apply policy for freshness, approval, rate limits, and device authority before any equipment action.
  5. Verify. Record what happened, keeping command receipt, device reports, and independently observed effects separate.

In the cold-box example, the model can propose a cooling mode. It cannot choose motor power or run time. Those limits belong to policy and device firmware. An operational pilot would start with a read-only feed and recommendations for the team.

Learning from verified outcomes

Tamoz keeps outcome evidence so later decisions can refer to earlier situations. The pump trial also tested a more specific learning loop: repeated verified repairs could lead to a proposed rule.

A draft rule was replayed against earlier data, approved, and tested in shadow before becoming active. Once active, it could handle that pattern without another model call. In the trial, one of three fault families produced an approved rule. The trial used a scripted approver; human review in an operation remains to be tested.

In the trial, a repair counted as verified when the fault did not return within 24 simulated hours, so sensor drift misdiagnosed as bearing wear could also pass. A stricter recovery check has since been added. Replayed over the trial's records, it rejects those cases, but the trial has not been rerun with it.

What has been tested

A pump station with a real model

In one simulated eight-pump station, Tamoz worked through 18 injected faults with a real model, once with a learned rule and once without. The rule handled five misalignment faults with no model call, all correct and on time.

This was one simulated station and three test runs of 30 simulated hours each. Only one of three fault families earned a rule, and the trial approver was scripted. The rule reduced model calls but missed its target: 13 of 17 detected faults still needed the model, against a goal of half the comparison. It did not improve misalignment repairs. The model repeatedly misdiagnosed sensor drift. These results are limited to simulation and do not establish industrial performance or savings. A pilot would measure these same numbers on your equipment. Read the trial and its limits.

Simulator rounds across several domains

Earlier rounds exercised timing, approvals, memory, and recovery in scenarios including aquaculture, water networks, and cold chain. Their reasoning was scripted. They tested the surrounding machinery, rather than a model's ability to diagnose those situations. Read the simulator evidence.

From Tamoz to a real board

We have run the whole route on real hardware: from Tamoz's proposal, through policy and a person's approval, to a command on a small board with a fan motor. Three recorded sessions on 25 October 2026 took this path.

This was a bench test, not plant equipment. The owner approved each command, the thresholds were set for the bench, and confirmation came from the board's own report. With the motor disconnected, the board still reported success, so these sessions do not measure a physical effect such as cooling. That is one reason a pilot starts with monitoring and recommendations, and why any later equipment action would need an independent check, such as a temperature or current reading. Read the bench evidence or the earlier motor test.

What remains to be shown

Tamoz is pre-release software. There has been no field deployment, and we have not yet established savings in a real operation. The simulations do not establish safety certification, fault coverage, or reliability on industrial equipment.

The proposed pilot starts with monitoring and recommendations. The customer decides what to do. Any later equipment action would require its own agreed scope and safeguards; existing safety systems remain responsible for protection.
