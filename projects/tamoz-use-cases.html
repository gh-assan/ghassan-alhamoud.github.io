Where Tamoz fits: four use cases, and how far each one is proven

Use CasesPredictive MaintenanceThermal ZonesPilot

Tamoz fits where equipment already streams sensor data, a fault has a deadline and a known repair, and a wrong action costs more than a late one. Below are four places I think that is true, and for each one exactly how far it has been shown. I would rather lose a pilot to an honest label than win one with a vague claim.

How to read the labels

Every use case carries one or two evidence labels. Each label is a word, not only a colour.

Demonstrated in simulation ran in the simulator, with sealed ground truthDemonstrated on a real bench ran on real hardware, small scale, operator-observedPlausible next world or spec material exists, but no run yet

Design: what makes a good fit

  • The sensors already stream, or a gateway can be added.
  • A fault has a deadline and a known repair.
  • The repair can start as a low-risk, reversible action (a ticket, a work order, a bounded setpoint request), and a person approves anything riskier.
  • A false alarm has a cost worth measuring.
  • A named person can approve rules.

Poor fit: hard real-time control loops, and anything that is itself a safety function. Tamoz sits above the control layer, and the learned-rule compiler refuses to recommend actions at risk class R3 or higher.

Demonstrated in simulation

1. Pump and motor fleets: condition-based maintenance

Operator's problem
A fleet of pumps and motors drifts away from its own normal in many ways: bearing wear, misalignment, a drifting probe. Thresholds alarm. A person works out which repair to book before the damage deadline, and the lesson is rarely written down.
What the system does
It opens a situation when a pump drifts from its own 24-hour baseline. One bounded AI diagnosis picks the fault and books the maintenance. Policy checks it. The repair is checked for a real recovery. After three verified repairs of the same kind it drafts a rule for a person to approve. See rule promotion.
What exists
The pump-station trial: eight simulated pumps, a real model, and a learned misalignment rule that made 5 of 5 right and on-time decisions with no false action. The simulated motor in the predictive-maintenance example, the first acceptance proof (deterministic replay, duplicate and out-of-order handling, hysteresis, episode cancellation, governed intents, idempotent effects, shadow mode, explainability). A rotating-machinery spec for bearing degradation. Limit: one fault family earned a rule; sensor drift is misdiagnosed as bearing wear.
A pilot would measure
Time from first drift to booked work order. The share of faults with the right repair booked before the deadline. False actions on healthy machines (target zero). Model calls per handled fault. Per fault family, whether a rule was earned and whether replay agreed. The share of repairs verified as recovered. Approver time per proposal.

2. Thermal zones, cold rooms and small actuators

Operator's problem
A cold box or climate zone drifts warm at night. The cause may be the door, the room, or a failing sensor, and the actuator (a fan or cooler) must never run unbounded.
What the system does
It reads temperature and humidity (and ambient or heartbeat where present) and discounts a rise that tracks the ambient. It answers with a mode, hold or bounded_cooling, never a power value. Policy and firmware set duty, lease and safe stop (a 60% cap and a 10-second lease on the bench). Silent sensors make the situation uncertain, and risky actions fail closed.
What exists
The thermal-chamber example, with traces for a quiet night, an ambient-tracking rise, invalid quality and a reboot backlog. The real-world-sensor example, with simulated and bench specs. The bench is an ELEGOO Mega 2560, a DHT11 sensor, an L293D driver and a small DC motor with a fan. A real model chose bounded_cooling on the cold-box snapshot. Limit: rotation was operator-observed, not instrumented, and the full route from Tamoz's intent to the motor is still an open integration seam. See the hardware bench.
A pilot would measure
Detection delay for a sustained excursion. Time to a bounded response after approval. False responses on ambient-tracking rises. Behaviour when a sensor goes silent (it must fail closed). Instrumented feedback, such as airflow or a tachometer, so that “energised” is not mistaken for “spinning”.
Demonstrated in simulation (scripted reasoner)

3. Aquaculture ponds: oxygen with a hard deadline

Operator's problem
Dissolved oxygen falls through the night. Below roughly 2 mg/L stock starts dying within about an hour, so the chain from reading to action must finish inside a physical window the world does not extend.
What the system does
It watches eight ponds, tells a post-feeding dip from a real fall, waits for a signed human approval before the emergency water exchange, fails closed when the probe goes silent, and walks back an intervention after a late correction.
What exists
An aquaculture simulator domain with eight shrimp ponds. Round 3 of the simulator rounds: a quiet baseline admitted nothing, a silent probe failed closed, the emergency exchange waited for a signed approval, and a second pond recalled the first. An aquaculture action catalog shared between the Go and Ruby sides, with its digest pinned by a test. Limit: these runs used a scripted reasoner, not a real model. Aquaculture has not been run with a real model.
A pilot would measure
Reading-to-action time against the physical window. Approvals that arrive in time. False interventions. Behaviour under sensor silence.

4. Chilled-water and utility plants

Operator's problem
Utilities (compressed air, chilled water, power) have faults that look alike across subsystems: an air leak against airend wear, a tower-fan failure against a heat wave, a stale main meter against its sub-meters.
What the system would do
The same loop. The value is in separating look-alikes and in not acting on the wrong one.
What exists
The plant can already be modelled in the simulator: a chiller pilot plant (chillers and an air-handling unit) and a factory-utilities plant (23 instances in five areas: site, production hall, compressor room, chiller plant and substation), shown below. Limit: there is no Tamoz run on either, so this stays “plausible next”.
A pilot would measure
The same measures as use case 1, plus a look-alike confusion count: how often one fault is called another.

A pilot, step by step

This is a proposal; it has not been done with anyone yet. Terms are to be discussed.

  1. Rehearse. Replay a sample of your sensor history through the system and, where useful, build a simulated copy of the device. Nothing touches equipment.
  2. Advise. It runs beside your people on a live, read-only feed and proposes repairs. Nobody acts on them unless they choose to. Every proposal and its evidence is logged.
  3. Act within bounds. Low-risk, reversible actions with a named approver, and learned rules only after your approval, in shadow first.

Before any pilot touches equipment I can run your data and a simulated copy of your device in the simulator; the same session can later put your real board beside simulated ones. That path is built and tested against a stand-in gateway; I have not yet run it with a real board.

Boundary: where it does not fit, and what it will not claim

Read before you rely on it

No safety function, no hard real-time control, no certification. Nothing here is a savings claim. The results above come from simulation or a small bench, and the evidence is on the overview and in the technical deep dive.
